How Long a Claim Appeal Takes, by Payer

Five appeal clocks run at once in American health care, and they do not agree with each other. The timetable for Medicare, Medicare Advantage, and commercial payers — and the cost of each month of waiting.

Prime Domain Capital InsightsNovember 19, 2026

The denial arrived on a Tuesday. The appeal was filed on a Thursday. The money arrived eleven months later, in a lump sum, with no interest and no apology — and by then the hospital had already borrowed to cover the gap. Nobody in the revenue cycle disputes that the appeal was worth filing. The question nobody asked until the cash-flow report came out was simpler: how long does each payer actually take, and what does each month of waiting cost?

The answer depends entirely on which payer denied the claim, because American health care runs five different appeal clocks at once, and they do not agree with each other. A Medicare fee-for-service claim, a Medicare Advantage claim, a commercial claim, and a Medicaid claim can all be denied on the same day for the same service, and the four appeals will resolve months apart — if they resolve at all. The hospitals that manage this well do not appeal faster in general. They appeal each payer on that payer's own timetable, and they price the wait.

Start with Original Medicare, where the rules are the most precise and the most forgiving. A provider has 120 days from receipt of the initial determination to request a redetermination from the Medicare Administrative Contractor, and the contractor has 60 days to decide. Lose there, and the provider has 180 days to seek reconsideration from a Qualified Independent Contractor, which also has 60 days to decide. The third level — an Administrative Law Judge hearing through the Office of Medicare Hearings and Appeals — must be requested within 60 days, carries a $200 minimum amount in controversy for 2026, and is decided within 90 days. Two more levels follow, the Medicare Appeals Council and federal court, each with a 60-day filing window; judicial review requires $1,960 in controversy for 2026. A Medicare appeal that runs the full course can therefore take well over a year, but every step has a published deadline, and a contractor that misses its own deadline must offer escalation to the next level.

The recoupment clock runs separately, and it is the one that hurts. For an overpayment demand, the provider has 120 days to request redetermination — but recoupment begins on day 41 unless a valid redetermination request is filed by day 30. A hospital that treats the 120-day deadline as its real deadline will watch the money leave on day 41 while its appeal is still being drafted. The 30-day filing is the deadline that matters, and it is the single most valuable date in the revenue cycle.

Medicare Advantage: faster on paper, slower in practice

Medicare Advantage runs a different process, and the difference matters more than the similarity. The first level is a reconsideration handled by the plan itself, not an independent contractor, and providers have 60 days to request it. The plan must decide within 30 days for services not yet received and 60 days for payment disputes; expedited requests get 72 hours. If the plan upholds the denial, the case goes automatically to an Independent Review Entity. That automatic forwarding is the structural advantage of the Advantage process — the hospital does not have to file the second appeal itself — but the first appeal is decided by the organization that issued the denial, and the June 2026 OIG reports show how that plays out: across the 19 largest Medicare Advantage organizations, plans denied 12 percent of skilled nursing facility requests and overturned 95 percent of those denials when patients appealed.

The overturn rate is the strongest evidence in the file, and it is also the reason the timeline matters so much. Only about one-third of patients appealed their denials in the OIG review. The rest never entered the process at all, which means the effective timeline for most denied care is not measured in days. It is never. For the hospital, the lesson is that the appeal must be filed as a matter of routine, not as a judgment call about whether the case is winnable — a 95 percent overturn rate on appealed skilled nursing denials is not a close call.

Commercial payers: the 180-day window and the 72-hour rule

Commercial claims run on the timetable Congress wrote into the Affordable Care Act. A provider or patient has 180 days to request an internal appeal, and the plan must decide within 30 days for pre-service claims and 60 days for post-service claims. Urgent care gets 72 hours. If the internal appeal fails, the claimant has four months to request an external review by an independent review organization, which must decide within 45 days — 72 hours if expedited. The external reviewer's decision binds the plan.

The commercial timeline looks generous next to Medicare's, and in one way it is: 180 days to file is the longest first-level window in the system. The catch is that the plan's own medical policy, not Medicare's coverage rules, governs the decision, and the documentation standard is whatever the plan's policy says it is. The appeal that wins a commercial denial is usually the one that quotes the plan's own policy back to it, with the clinical note attached. Appeals that argue fairness lose. Appeals that argue the policy lose less often.

What each month of delay actually costs

The cash-flow cost of an appeal is easy to understate and easy to compute. Take a mid-size hospital with $5 million in appealed claims outstanding at any given time — a modest figure for a hospital that appeals routinely. At an 8 percent cost of capital, each month those claims sit unpaid costs roughly $33,000 in carrying cost alone, before staff time. Stretch the average appeal from three months to nine, and the carrying cost triples to nearly $200,000 a year, for money the hospital has already earned. The figure is illustrative, not a benchmark, but the direction is not in dispute: delay is a cost, and it compounds exactly like interest, because it is interest.

That is why the fastest hospitals do not measure appeal success by overturn rate alone. They measure days in accounts receivable by payer, and they staff to the payer whose clock runs slowest. A Medicare Advantage reconsideration that takes 60 days and an external review that takes 45 more are, together, a quarter of lost cash flow on that claim. A commercial internal appeal decided in 30 days is half that. The appeal strategy that follows is unglamorous and effective: file within days, not weeks; attach the complete record the first time, because a request for additional information restarts the payer's clock; and escalate the moment a deadline passes, because the escalation right is the only leverage a hospital has over a payer that is late.

The counterargument: the timelines exist for a reason

Payers will say, with some justification, that appeal timelines protect accuracy. A redetermination decided in 60 days is a paper review by staff who did not make the original decision; a QIC reconsideration adds an independent reviewer; an ALJ hearing adds testimony and a fresh look at the evidence. Each level is slower because each level is more thorough, and the system is designed to let weak denials die early and strong ones survive scrutiny. The 95 percent overturn rate on skilled nursing denials, on this reading, is the system working — the appeals process catching errors the initial review missed.

That reading has a limit, and it is the same limit the OIG drew in June 2026: a system that denies first and corrects only for the minority who appeal is not sorting necessary from unnecessary care. It is rationing by attrition. The hospitals that accept the timelines as given, file on day one, and treat the appeal as part of the billing process rather than an exception to it, are the ones that get paid. The ones that treat the denial as the end of the conversation are the ones that finance the payers' float.
